Before the Code: Building the Factory Behind the Sierra Cosworth

By Rick Adams

Go to any classic car show and you’ll find a crowd around it — the whale-tail spoiler, the flared arches, that unmistakable silhouette. The Ford Sierra RS Cosworth is a legend now, celebrated in YouTube retrospectives, magazine features, and TV documentaries. They all tell the story of the car, and the YB engine that made it immortal.

There’s one part almost nobody tells: how it was actually made. Not the engineering drawings — the factory. The production line. The systems that turned a motorsport engine into something you could build five thousand of, every year, without ever compromising what made it special.

I know that part, because I was there to build it.

In the early 1980s, Ford set out to create a high-performance Sierra to homologate for Group A touring car racing. They called Cosworth. And when the project started, I was one of the two original engineers assigned to it.

This is a departure from what I write about now — today I run Rivendell AI, a software company. But before software, I had a different engineering life. And the way I learned to think back then still shapes everything I do today.

The challenge: a race engine you could mass-produce

Ford’s ask was deceptively simple and genuinely hard: a homologation engine that could win in Group A, but also live under the bonnet of a road car — all the power of a motorsport unit, with the reliability and manufacturability of a mass-produced one.

Cosworth built race engines. Brilliant ones. But we had never produced anything at the scale and volume Ford needed. This wasn’t just about designing a great engine. We had to design the means to build it — at volume, without giving an inch on quality.

Building the facility from a blank sheet

Once the project was approved, we secured a new building in Wellingborough, Northamptonshire, as the dedicated production facility for the Sierra Cosworth engine. Outfitting it was no small task: selecting and acquiring every piece of machining equipment to hit both our performance and volume targets — and leaving enough room, of course, for the Cosworth helicopter to ferry visitors in.

The work split naturally between the two of us. Pete Bartram took the production-engineering side — machining processes, fixture design, tooling strategy. I took the systems side: specifying, procuring, and integrating everything needed to actually run a modern engine line. We leaned on Cosworth HQ’s infrastructure where we could, and built new where we had to.

That meant architecting the operational backbone of the whole plant:

  • MRP (Material Requirements Planning) — just-in-time visibility into the parts pipeline and supplier flow
  • Hot test systems — validating engine performance immediately after assembly: every unit, every time
  • SPC (Statistical Process Control) — data-driven quality, monitoring machining precision for consistency
  • Quality control — inspection routines, defect tracking, root-cause analysis
  • Payroll — because even great engineers need to get paid on time
  • Internal comms & IT — from wiring the network to linking the shop floor to management reporting

It wasn’t just installing software. We were building the physical line and, at the same time, the digital nervous system to run it. An operational startup inside an engineering company.

And somewhere in that phase, something unexpected happened to me. Surrounded by MRP, hot-test diagnostics, data flows, and early business logic, I realised I was more drawn to the systems that made everything work than to the machining itself. What started as a necessity — wiring together the tools to support engine production — became a fascination with how digital systems solve hard operational problems. That was the turning point that eventually carried me from production engineer to IT and, ultimately, software. The road to Rivendell AI started on that shop floor.

A crash course in leadership

One moment stuck with me for the rest of my career. I was called into the office of our Managing Director, Alf Vickers. He sat me down and said something I’ve never forgotten:

“When we’re deciding on equipment, buildings, processes, or systems, I want you to give me a truthful, deep analysis of the options — the pros and the cons. But once a decision is made, even if it’s not the one you’d have picked, you back it 100%. Because I may be working with factors you don’t know about.”

It was my first real taste of executive-level engineering decisions — rigorous analysis, followed by total commitment. Not every call was neat. I remember late nights at Cosworth with Mike Costin, Keith Duckworth, Benny Rood, Alf, Pete and me, having loud, passionate arguments over different stages of the project. These weren’t casual chats. They were fierce debates between people who cared, deeply, about getting it right.

Another chapter I won’t forget: choosing a machine-tool supplier. I met reps from KTM and Heller — not in factories, but in hotel rooms, each one making their case for why their technology was the right bet for our line. For a young engineer stepping into high-stakes purchasing, it was intense and completely absorbing.

Scaling up: 5,000 engines, and a side of Mercedes

Once Wellingborough was running, the target was clear: 5,000 Sierra Cosworth engines a year. Machining Cosworth-designed aluminium cylinder heads, assembling complete engines, and hot-testing every single one before it left the building. Precision production at that volume was a first for Cosworth, and it forced us to rethink how we manufactured.

And to keep things interesting, we were also contracted to machine 2,500 twin-cam, four-valve cylinder heads for the Mercedes-Benz 190 — another Group A contender. Running both programmes in parallel, each with its own materials and tolerances, put real pressure on planning, tooling, and resources.

That’s exactly why we adopted FMS — Flexible Manufacturing Systems. Instead of a rigid, single-purpose line, we built a setup that could adapt fast to different head designs and batch sizes: smarter fixtures, automated tool changes, programmable CNC, intelligent scheduling — all run through the systems we’d integrated from the ground up. One shift could be Sierra production; the next, Mercedes heads — with minimal downtime between. For a traditional race-engine builder, that was a serious leap into advanced manufacturing. And it worked.

Growing the team, and going live

What began with just Pete and me grew into a full production and engineering operation. Among the people we brought in was Bernard Ferguson, who would later become Cosworth’s Director of Motorsport, overseeing some of the most prestigious programmes in Formula 1 and beyond. Having someone of his calibre on the Sierra project said everything about how strategically important this engine was to the company’s future.

It took two full years — planning, building, specifying, debugging, across facilities, systems, and people. And then, one day, everything was in place. We entered production. Heads being machined, engines assembled, tested, shipped. The systems we’d spent so long designing were running, day in and day out. Concept and setup had become a living, high-performance manufacturing operation.

A moment to remember

One of the best came when Pete and I got to drive the Sierra Cosworth test car ourselves. It was a raw, unfiltered machine, and we couldn’t resist finding out what it could really do. We found a long enough stretch of road, put a foot down — and it delivered. We spun the wheels in fourth gear, hit 140 mph, and decided we’d probably made our point.

For two engineers who’d spent years designing, building, and debugging that engine, it was pure satisfaction. Everything we’d worked for — the power, the response, the precision — all of it, real, under us, at speed.

Looking back

From a blank sheet of paper to a fully operational engine line, that project was one of the most formative experiences of my career. It pushed me out of traditional production engineering and into systems, IT, and eventually software. Everything I learned about building integrated systems, managing complexity, and delivering under pressure still guides how I work.

At Rivendell AI the problems are different — software, data, automation instead of aluminium and CNC — but the principles haven’t changed:

Understand the system. Design it properly. Be honest in your analysis. And once the decision is made, back it 100%.

The Sierra Cosworth wasn’t just a great car. It was a great lesson in engineering, in teamwork, and in what it takes to turn an ambitious idea into something real. I’ve been doing that ever since — I just build it in code now.
